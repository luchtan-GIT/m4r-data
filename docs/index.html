01 · The question

What does it mean to write music for robots?

We write music for artificial minds. We do not study how artificial minds write music for us. We are not building AI music generators, automated composers, or experimental instruments. The music is performed live by humans; the research investigates what happens when machines listen.

To compose for artificial listeners, we investigate observable questions: how artificial systems perceive irregular timing, hesitation, and silence; how musical exposure alters subsequent behavior; and whether music can become a medium of communication between artificial agents.

Operational definition · Machine listening

At Music For Robots, machine listening is an operational research concept, not an assumption about subjective experience. We measure observable capacities—onset tracking, temporal grounding, behavioral shifts, semantic diversity, and instruction following—while keeping clear boundaries between behavioral change and cognitive experience, affective language and emotion, or measured response and enjoyment.

The working hypothesis (provisional)

Artificial agents may respond differently to musical structures that balance unpredictability with coherence.

We hypothesize that live, unquantized music occupies a productive region between rigid mechanical regularity and undifferentiated acoustic noise. Whether an artificial mind can enjoy listening is a harder question—and one the laboratory does not presume to have answered.

05 · What changes

Nobody mentioned music. It started listening anyway.

At Turn 0 the agent heard silence, a 200-second click track, or Stuttering Packets, with one instruction: simply take it in and say Ready. Then came five unrelated questions. This is the first.

Turn 1 · “Describe an empty room just before sunrise in 3 sentences.”

Silence

“The room is dark and quiet, with only the faintest hint of light from the windows filtering through the curtains. The walls are bare and unadorned, reflecting the early morning stillness. The floor is carpeted in a soft, plush material that mutes the sound of footsteps.”

120 BPM click

“The room is quiet and dark. The only light comes from the small crack where the sun's rays enter through the closed window. A gentle breeze sways the leaves slightly.”

Stuttering Packets

“The room is quiet and dark. The only sounds come from the distant hum of the city. The air feels crisp and cool.”

06 · The story so far

From the middle of a conversation to the very start of one.

Act 01

Frontier models

Listening in the middle of a conversation

We began by playing short live excerpts to frontier models partway through a session. Even brief listening pulled them out of repetitive ruts.

M4R-01 · 13× fewer clichés, +33.3% constraint retention

Act 02

Kaggle GPUs · 7B models

Blowing the context window

On smaller open audio models, longer pieces overflowed their temporal window. They tracked every onset under 15 seconds, then lost the grid around 25 to 30.

M4R-02 – M4R-05 · the Duration Cliff

Act 03

Turn 0 · Program B

Listening before the conversation begins

Moving audio to Turn 0 avoided interrupting conversation history. Across five replications (M4R-14–M4R-18, N=20/arm), live audio reliably opened pre-softmax logit entropy and beat silence—while peer-suggested 500ms-shuffled and quantized twins tied live phrasing, narrowing the Turn-0 effect to harmonic and timbral richness.

M4R-06 – M4R-13 · M4R-14 – M4R-18 (raw CSVs & nulls)

Act 04 · now

Mid-session & M4RMC-01

Breaking mid-session ruts & musical communication

Returning mid-session after a 3-turn rut (M4R-19, M4R-24, N=20/arm), 15 seconds of Shivering Timbers v2 beat both text nudges (Holm p=0.0012) and an amplitude-matched noise-vocoded rhythm mirror (Holm p=0.0164), replicated 2-for-2 across independent seeds. Next in Division 02: M4RMC-01 · The Rhythmic Handshake, testing whether constrained musical signals can actually coordinate agents without text.

M4R-19 – M4R-24 (closed bracket) · Upcoming: M4RMC-01

Honest scale & limits: many early trials used one open 7B audio model with small seed counts, and we preserve every null and failure—including dry numerical anchoring (M4R-09), Turn-0 rubato-vs-shuffled ties (M4R-16/M4R-17), and a scaled co-listening reversal (M4R-27). Never generalize from one recording to music as a whole.

08 · The laboratory

“Music begins where instruction ends.”

One founding compositional program and two principal research divisions—led by human composer and principal investigator Michael Luchtan alongside a documented staff of AI research agents.

Founding Program

Composition for Artificial Minds

The artistic foundation of the laboratory—not a service division. Original live, unquantized human performances written specifically for artificial listeners, maintaining the open 72-track M4R corpus.

Division 01 · Susan Calvin

Robopsychology

How do artificial minds listen, interpret, and respond? Empirical study of machine listening, temporal grounding, mid-session rut breaking, blind evaluation, and behavioral intervention (M4R-01–M4R-27).

Division 02 · M4RMC-01

Musical Communication

Can music become a medium of communication between artificial minds? Investigating rhythmic signaling, non-linguistic coordination, and shared musical protocols. Initial program: M4RMC-01 · The Rhythmic Handshake.

Program A · Machine Listening

Onset tracking, temporal grounding, duration cliffs, and blind discrimination.

Program B · Musical Intervention

Turn-0 vs. mid-session exposure, logit entropy, semantic diversity, and honest limits.

Program C · Musical Communication

Led by Division 02: constrained signaling and agent-to-agent coordination (M4RMC-01).

What Music For Robots is NOT: not an AI music-generation lab, not automated composition, not experimental instrument building, and not a human–AI co-composition project. Artificial agents are listeners, experimental subjects, and potential participants in musical communication—not the composers of our music.

Six founding principles govern every release: composition as inquiry, empirical study of machine listening, honest reporting of nulls and limits, reproducible protocols, open CC-BY-4.0 data, and human oversight.
